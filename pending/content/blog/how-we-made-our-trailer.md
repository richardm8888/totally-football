Board games are hard to show on camera.

Nothing moves on its own. The drama of a match lives in the players' heads: the bluff before a duel, the groan when a chaos card lands, the celebration when a last-minute shot goes in.

So when we made a trailer for [Totally Football, our tactical football board game](/), the challenge was clear: how do you make cards on a table feel like a football match?

Here's how we did it.

## The idea: football drama

A trailer was always part of the plan. From the very start of the project we knew we wanted one, and Sean had a clear vision for it.

That vision came down to one idea: **football drama**.

Totally Football is built to give you the same emotions as playing the real game. The tension of a one-on-one, the swing of momentum, the celebration when it all goes your way. We wanted anyone watching to understand that within the first ten seconds.


## Planning every shot

Sean is a videographer by trade, and he planned the trailer meticulously before we filmed a single frame.

That planning mattered, because there was a lot to cover. A board game trailer needs a huge number of shots and angles: the cards, the board, the players, their reactions. Without a plan, it would have been easy to finish a long day of filming and still be missing the moment that ties a scene together.


## Two locations, one match

We split the shoot across two very different places.

The first was **an astro turf pitch**, where we spent about three hours capturing the real football side of the story.

The second was **a family house**, where we spent another three to four hours filming the game being played around the table.

Our cast were friends and playtesters, people who already knew the game and genuinely get competitive when they play it. That made a big difference. The reactions on camera are real.


## Filming fast-moving action

This was the part that stretched Sean the most.

Much of his usual work involves reasonably static subjects. A football match is the opposite. On the pitch, the action is fast and unpredictable, and you only get one chance to catch the moment. Keeping up with that, and then matching its energy back at the table, was the biggest challenge of the shoot.


## The edit: choosing one story

We filmed three or four different match scenarios, each with its own storyline.

In the end, only one made it into the trailer as the main story. It was the strongest and the clearest, and a trailer only has time to tell one story well.

The other scenarios weren't wasted. Small snippets from them appear in a montage, adding pace and variety without pulling focus from the main story.

The music is licensed, which Sean had access to through his work, so the trailer could have a soundtrack that matches the drama on screen.


## Our favourite moment

If we had to pick one shot, it's the close-up of Dan, our goalkeeper, celebrating.

It sums up exactly what we wanted the trailer to show: a board game creating a reaction you'd normally only see on a football pitch.


## What we learned

If you're an indie game designer making your first trailer, here's what we'd pass on:

1. **Plan meticulously.** Know every shot before you start filming. It saves time on the day and makes sure you capture everything the edit needs.
2. **Allow far more time than you think.** Filming took much longer than we expected, even with a detailed plan.
3. **Film more than you need.** We shot three or four scenarios and used one. The extra footage gave us choices in the edit and material for the montage.
4. **Cast people who love the game.** Real reactions from friends and playtesters beat anything you could direct.
